## CHALLENGE

![](./01.png)

**Which product concept fits an entry into the U.S. market?**

[**ZIBOT Inc.**](https://www.zhibankeji.com/) is a China-based startup that builds educational robots for children using natural language analysis. In 2018, having validated its product in the Chinese market, the company set up a Silicon Valley office to enter the United States, but it had little data or insight about that market at the time. The U.S. product team's main task, then, was to gather concrete data and insight on the U.S. market and find the opportunity areas that fit it.

![](./02.png)

![](./03.png)


## APPROACH

**Researching the U.S. kids robot market and its products :** Through desk research I catalogued every kids robot on the U.S. market in a spreadsheet — manufacturer, specs, main features, interactions, strengths and weaknesses. I then bought and used the major products myself and wrote a report analyzing market trends, product characteristics, and opportunity areas.

**Idea workshop (brainstorming) :** With the CEO and the PM, I ran an idea workshop that pulled key words out of the market and product analysis and combined them in different ways. From the results we produced several early concepts reflecting the features and design elements U.S. customers were likely to prefer.

**Product concept design and development :** We picked the most promising of those ideas and developed a product prototype, visual design included. From early sketches through 3D modeling to a physical prototype, the design process defined the final product's form and functions.

## ZIBOT KK

**Learning to code through color recognition (color coding) :** So that children can pick up coding principles through color patterns, the robot recognizes colors and performs the matching action. The approach is intuitive and fun, and it builds an understanding of coding concepts along the way.

**A modular handle for extensibility :** The robot's handle is modular and takes a variety of accessories. That extends its use beyond education to play and exploration.

**A small, cute design :** A small, cute design helps children feel close to the robot. The look is appealing and gives visual pleasure without giving up functional usefulness.

## IMPLEMENTATION

**Validating the design concept through prototyping :** To meet the 'small and cute' image the concept called for, I built prototypes repeatedly. Trying different sizes and forms showed what product size was feasible and refined the design toward something close to a real product. Prototyping also verified the design's function and usability, and led to an optimized form.

**Testing feasibility with Arduino and MIT App Inventor :** To check whether the color-coding feature would actually work, I built and tested a prototype of the core function using Arduino and MIT App Inventor. The early tests revealed functional constraints and room for improvement, and became reference material for later product development. The process confirmed early on that color-recognition-based interaction was technically feasible.

**A CES 2019 exhibition mockup, built with the design team:** Working from what repeated prototyping and testing had shown, I collaborated closely with Frank, the design team lead at the Chinese headquarters, to settle the final product design concept. From that concept we produced a mockup for the CES 2019 exhibition so that visitors could see and handle the product's form and functions on site. This wrapped up the detailed design and let us show the physical product to people in the industry.

## IMPACT

**Exhibited at CES 2019:** The final ZIBOT KK mockup was shown at ZIBOT's booth at CES 2019. It was the first project the U.S. product team completed, and the starting point for the company's entry into the U.S. market.

## REFLECTION

**A demanding environment can turn into a chance to learn, once you get through it.**

The internship in Silicon Valley in 2018 was nothing like what I had expected. I started at a small U.S. branch of a Chinese startup where the team was two people, a CTO and a PM, and team building and process were only just beginning. For me, an undergraduate at the time, it was a very demanding situation, and honestly I was disappointed. The Silicon Valley I had pictured was nowhere in sight. But I had wanted this chance more than anyone, and I did not want to spend the time that way, so I started working through the problems one at a time.

First, I leaned on visuals and documentation to get past the communication barrier with the Chinese headquarters. Then I fixed inefficient processes myself and put the team's workflow in order. I also put the team's goals ahead of my own career goals and took on the work I was given. That effort paid off: I moved from an unpaid intern to a paid contract role and got another year with the team. The company then started a new project based on my work, and I took part in product planning and design in earnest, with real responsibility.

From this I learned that a demanding environment brings bigger lessons, and that the difficulty in front of you can turn out to be an opportunity.
